## Objectives

- Explain the difference between **common law** (England and Wales) and **civil law** (Spain).
- Understand the role of **precedent** and **statute**.
- Know how **juries** work in the Crown Court and how that differs from Spain.

## Explanation

### Two legal families

| | **Common law** (England & Wales) | **Civil law** (Spain) |
|---|---|---|
| Main source | **Statutes** (Acts of Parliament) **and case law** (judges' decisions) | **Codes** (e.g. *Código Penal*) and statutes |
| Role of judges | Their decisions create **precedent** binding on lower courts | They apply the code; *jurisprudencia* is important but codes are central |
| Trial | **Adversarial**: prosecution vs defence, judge as referee | More **inquisitorial** elements: an investigating judge (*juez de instrucción*) |
| Investigation | **Police** investigate; **CPS** decides charge in most cases | Judge/prosecutor direct the investigation |

Some English offences are still **common law offences** (defined by judges, not by an Act), e.g. **murder**, **manslaughter**, **misconduct in public office**, **conspiracy to defraud**. Their **sentences** may be set by statute.

:::es
- **Common law**: leyes del Parlamento **+ decisiones judiciales** que crean **precedente** (obligatorio para tribunales inferiores). Juicio **adversarial**.
- **Derecho continental (España)**: **códigos** (Código Penal) y leyes; juez de instrucción.
- Algunos delitos ingleses **no están en ninguna ley**: los definieron los jueces (**asesinato, homicidio imprudente, misconduct in public office**). Por eso estudiarás muchos **casos** (*R v …*).
:::

### Reading case names

- ***R v Smith*** = *Regina* (the Crown) **versus** Smith → a criminal prosecution. Say "the Crown against Smith" or "R and Smith".
- ***DPP v Smith*** = Director of Public Prosecutions v Smith (often an appeal).
- The **year** helps place the case: *R v Woollin (1998)*.

:::es
***R v Smith*** = "la Corona contra Smith" (R = *Regina/Rex*). ***DPP v …*** = Director de la Fiscalía contra… (suele ser un recurso).
:::

### Juries

- Used in the **Crown Court** for trials of **indictable** and **either-way** offences sent there, when the defendant pleads **not guilty**.
- **12 jurors**, members of the public aged **18 to 75** on the electoral register (⚠ VERIFICAR requisitos actuales).
- The **judge** decides questions of **law** and the **sentence**; the **jury** decides the **facts** – **guilty or not guilty**.
- The jury should aim for a **unanimous** verdict; after a minimum time the judge may accept a **majority verdict** (e.g. **10–2** or **11–1**) (Juries Act 1974, s. 17).
- **Magistrates' courts** have **no jury**: lay magistrates or a district judge decide.

In **Spain** the *Tribunal del Jurado* has **9 jurors** and is used only for certain offences (e.g. some homicides) – much less common.

:::es
- **Jurado** en la **Crown Court**: **12 personas** (18–75 años en el censo ⚠ verificar), decide **culpable o no culpable**; el **juez** decide el **derecho** y la **pena**.
- Se busca unanimidad; después de un tiempo mínimo se admite **mayoría** (10–2 o 11–1).
- En el **magistrates' court** no hay jurado.
- En España el jurado tiene **9 miembros** y solo para algunos delitos.
:::

## Police scenarios

1. You read *"R v Jogee [2016] UKSC 8"* in the manual. → A Supreme Court decision (2016) that binds all lower courts – it is **precedent**.
2. A defendant pleads not guilty to burglary at the Crown Court. → A **jury** will hear the evidence, including **your** evidence as a witness.

## Common mistakes & traps

- Thinking all crimes are in one **code** – English criminal law is spread across many Acts **and** case law.
- Thinking the judge decides guilt in the Crown Court – the **jury** does.
- *Jurisprudence* in English usually means **legal theory**, not "case law". Use **case law**.

## Summary

1. Common law = statutes + case law (precedent).
2. Some offences are common law offences (murder, manslaughter).
3. Adversarial system; police investigate, CPS prosecutes.
4. Crown Court jury: 12 jurors decide facts; majority 10–2 or 11–1 possible.
5. Case names: *R v Smith* = the Crown against Smith.
