## Objectives

- Understand **why** most British officers are **unarmed** and what equipment they do carry.
- Know that there is **no compulsory national ID card** and how identity is checked in practice.
- Adapt your expectations as someone trained in the Spanish system.

## Explanation

### Mostly unarmed

In England and Wales, **routine officers do not carry firearms**. Only **Authorised Firearms Officers (AFOs)** – a small minority, e.g. in **Armed Response Vehicles (ARVs)** – carry guns, and only after specialist training and authorisation.

Typical **personal safety equipment**: **handcuffs**, **baton** (usually extendable), **PAVA incapacitant spray**, **body-worn video**, radio, and – for officers trained and authorised – **Taser** (a conducted energy device). Use of force must always be **necessary, proportionate and reasonable**, and must be **recorded**.

This links to **policing by consent** (lesson SE1): the public accepts the police partly because they are not a heavily armed force.

:::es
La mayoría de agentes **no lleva arma de fuego**. Solo los **AFO** (agentes autorizados, p. ej. en vehículos de respuesta armada **ARV**) la llevan. Equipo habitual: **esposas, defensa extensible, spray PAVA, cámara corporal, radio** y, si estás formada y autorizada, **Taser**. Toda fuerza debe ser **necesaria, proporcionada y razonable**, y se **registra**.
:::

### No national ID card

The UK has **no compulsory national identity card** and people are **not generally required to carry ID** or to tell the police who they are. In practice:

- People **choose** to show a **driving licence**, **passport**, bank card, etc.
- In some situations the law **does** require details: e.g. a **driver** must give name and address and produce a licence in certain circumstances; an officer can require **name and address** from a person acting in an **anti-social manner** (Police Reform Act 2002, s. 50 – ⚠ VERIFICAR detalles en la academia).
- Not knowing who someone is can make an **arrest necessary** (PACE Code G – to ascertain name and address).
- Checks are done on the **Police National Computer (PNC)** and other systems.

:::es
**No hay DNI obligatorio** y, en general, la gente **no tiene que llevar documentación** ni decir quién es. En la práctica: enseñan **carné de conducir o pasaporte** si quieren; en algunos casos la ley **sí** obliga (conductores; conducta antisocial – PRA 2002 s. 50 ⚠ verificar); y **no saber el nombre** puede hacer **necesaria** una detención (Code G). Las comprobaciones se hacen en el **PNC**.
:::

## Police scenarios

1. You stop a pedestrian under a stop and search power and ask for their name. They refuse. → For the search itself they don't have to give it; record it as **refused**. Think about whether any power requires details or whether an arrest becomes **necessary** if an offence is suspected.
2. A violent incident with a knife is reported. → Uniformed officers contain the scene; **armed officers (ARV)** are requested via the control room.

:::es
1. Registro y la persona no da su nombre → no está obligada para el registro; anótalo como **"refused"**. Valora si alguna ley lo exige o si la detención se vuelve **necesaria**.
2. Incidente con cuchillo → contener y pedir **ARV** (armados) por radio.
:::

## Common mistakes & traps

- Saying *"Show me your documentation"* as an order → ✅ *"Have you got any ID on you?"* (polite request).
- Assuming refusing to give a name is always an offence – it isn't, generally.
- Thinking Taser is carried by everyone – only **trained and authorised** officers.

## Summary

1. Most officers are unarmed; AFOs/ARVs carry firearms.
2. Equipment: cuffs, baton, PAVA, BWV, (Taser if authorised).
3. Force: necessary, proportionate, reasonable – and recorded.
4. No compulsory ID card; no general duty to carry ID.
5. Some laws require details; unknown identity can make arrest necessary.
