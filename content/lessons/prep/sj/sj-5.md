## Objectives

- Classify offences as **summary**, **either-way** or **indictable-only** and know where they are tried.
- Explain the **burden of proof** (who must prove) and the **standard of proof** (how sure).
- Know what a **reverse burden** is.

## Explanation

### Classification of offences

| Type | Where tried | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Summary only** | **Magistrates' court** only | common assault, most minor motoring offences, drunk and disorderly |
| **Either-way** | Magistrates' court **or** Crown Court | theft, most burglaries, ABH (s. 47), fraud, possession of drugs |
| **Indictable only** | **Crown Court** (after first appearance in magistrates) | murder, manslaughter, robbery, rape, GBH with intent (s. 18) |

For **either-way** offences there is an **allocation** hearing: the magistrates decide if the case is suitable for them; if they accept it, the defendant can still **elect** trial by jury in the Crown Court. "**Indictable offences**" in a wide sense include both indictable-only and either-way (Interpretation Act 1978 – ⚠ useful for PACE powers like superintendent's extensions).

:::es
- **Summary only**: solo **magistrados** (agresión simple, la mayoría de infracciones de tráfico menores).
- **Either-way** (mixtos): magistrados **o** Crown Court (hurto, la mayoría de *burglaries*, ABH, fraude, posesión de drogas). Hay una vista de **asignación** y el acusado puede **elegir jurado**.
- **Indictable only**: solo **Crown Court** (asesinato, homicidio, robo con violencia, violación, s. 18).
- "Indictable" en sentido amplio = indictable-only **+** either-way (importante para poderes de PACE).
:::

### Burden of proof

- The **prosecution** must prove the defendant's guilt: ***Woolmington v DPP* (1935)** – the "**golden thread**" of English criminal law.
- The defendant does **not** have to prove innocence. If they raise a defence (e.g. **self-defence**), the prosecution must **disprove** it.
- **Reverse burdens**: sometimes a statute puts a burden on the defendant, e.g. to prove a **good reason or lawful authority** for having a bladed article in public (Criminal Justice Act 1988, s. 139). The defendant then proves it **on the balance of probabilities**.

:::es
- La **acusación** debe probar la culpabilidad (*Woolmington v DPP* 1935: el "**hilo dorado**").
- El acusado **no** tiene que probar su inocencia; si alega una defensa (p. ej. legítima defensa), la acusación debe **desmontarla**.
- **Inversión de la carga**: a veces la ley exige al acusado probar algo (p. ej. **buena razón** para llevar un arma blanca, CJA 1988 s. 139), con el estándar del **balance de probabilidades**.
:::

### Standard of proof

- **Criminal**: the jury/magistrates must be **sure** of guilt ("**beyond reasonable doubt**").
- **Civil** (and reverse burdens on the defendant): **balance of probabilities** – more likely than not.
- **Charging** decisions use a different test: **realistic prospect of conviction** (SJ3).

:::es
- **Penal**: estar **seguros** de la culpabilidad (*beyond reasonable doubt*).
- **Civil** (y cargas invertidas): **balance de probabilidades** (más probable que no).
- Para **acusar** se usa otro test: **perspectiva realista de condena**.
:::

## Police scenarios

1. A suspect is arrested for **robbery** → indictable only → after first appearance at the magistrates' court it goes to the **Crown Court**.
2. A man with a knife in the street says he is a chef going to work. → Under s. 139 he must show **good reason** on the **balance of probabilities**.

## Common mistakes & traps

- Thinking robbery is either-way – it's **indictable only**.
- Confusing **burden** (who) with **standard** (how much).
- Thinking the defence must prove self-defence – the **prosecution** must disprove it.

## Summary

1. Summary / either-way / indictable only.
2. Either-way: allocation + defendant can elect jury.
3. Burden on the prosecution (Woolmington) – reverse burdens are exceptions.
4. Criminal standard: sure / beyond reasonable doubt.
5. Civil / reverse burden: balance of probabilities.
