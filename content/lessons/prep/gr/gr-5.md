## Objectives

- Understand the **legal meaning** of *may, must, shall, should, can, need not*.
- Read PACE Codes and laws precisely – the exam loves these words.
- Use modals correctly in your own reports and explanations.

## Explanation

| Modal | Legal meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **may** | **power / permission / discretion** – optional | *An officer **may** search a person…* (has the power, not the duty) |
| **may not** | not allowed | *A detainee **may not** be interviewed unless…* |
| **must** | **duty / obligation** | *The custody officer **must** inform the detainee…* |
| **shall** | **duty** (older legal style = must) | *A person **shall** be released if…* |
| **should** | **expected / recommended**; departing from it needs a **good reason** | *Interviews **should** be recorded…* |
| **need not / does not have to** | no obligation | *The person **need not** answer.* |
| **can** | ability or permission (less formal) | *You **can** ask for a solicitor at any time.* |
| **will** | future / strong expectation; in some texts like **must** | *The officer **will** record…* |

**Key rule for the exam:** *may* ≠ *must*. If a question says an officer **must** do something but the law says **may**, the answer is probably wrong.

:::es
- **may** = **puede** (facultad, no obligación). **may not** = no puede (prohibido).
- **must / shall** = **debe** (obligación). *Shall* es estilo legal antiguo = must.
- **should** = **debería** (lo esperado; para no hacerlo hace falta un buen motivo).
- **need not / does not have to** = **no tiene por qué**.
- ¡Ojo! **must not** = **prohibido**; **don't have to** = **no es obligatorio**. Son muy diferentes.
:::

### must not vs don't have to

- *You **must not** use your phone while driving.* → It's **prohibited**.
- *You **don't have to** answer questions.* → It's **optional**.

This difference is exactly what the **caution** says: *"You **do not have to** say anything…"*

:::es
**must not** = prohibido; **don't have to** = no obligatorio. La caution dice *"You **do not have to** say anything"*: no es obligatorio hablar, no está prohibido.
:::

### Possibility and deduction (useful in statements)

- *He **may/might** have been drinking.* (possible)
- *He **must** have been drinking – he smelt strongly of alcohol.* (strong deduction)
- *He **can't** have been driving – he was in custody.* (impossible)

In statements, keep to **facts**: prefer *"I smelt alcohol on his breath"* to *"he must have been drunk"*.

:::es
Deducción: *may/might have* (posible), *must have* (seguro, deducción), *can't have* (imposible). En declaraciones, mejor **hechos** que deducciones.
:::

## Police scenarios

1. A Code says the custody officer **may** authorise something. → It is a **discretion** – they can decide not to.
2. A Code says a review **shall** be carried out. → It is **mandatory**.
3. A Code says an appropriate adult **should** be present. → Expected; absence must be justified.

## Common mistakes (Spanish speakers)

- Translating *deber* always as **must**: *debería* = **should**.
- ❌ *You must not answer* (= prohibited!) when you mean ✅ *You **don't have to** answer*.
- ❌ *He must to go* → ✅ *He must go* (no *to* after modals; but *have to* and *need to* take *to*).

## Summary

1. may = power/discretion; must/shall = duty; should = expected.
2. must not = prohibited ≠ don't have to = optional.
3. Caution: "You do not have to say anything".
4. No *to* after modals (must go).
5. Exam trap: "must" vs "may".
