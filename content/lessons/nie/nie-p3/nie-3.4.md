## Objectives

- Distinguish the **two forms** of burglary: **s. 9(1)(a)** (entry with intent) and **s. 9(1)(b)** (having entered, steals or inflicts GBH).
- Understand **entry**, **building / part of a building** and **trespasser**.
- Know the **ulterior offences** and the **dwelling** rule for sentencing.

## Explanation

### 1. The two offences (Theft Act 1968, s. 9)

| | **s. 9(1)(a)** | **s. 9(1)(b)** |
|---|---|---|
| Actus reus | **enters** a building or part of a building **as a trespasser** | **having entered** as a trespasser… |
| Mental element / conduct | **with intent** to: **steal**, **inflict GBH** on any person, or do **unlawful damage** to the building or anything in it | …**steals or attempts to steal**, or **inflicts or attempts to inflict GBH** |
| Key moment | **at the time of entry** | **after** entry (what they actually do) |

Note: **criminal damage** is only an intent in **(a)**; it is **not** an act for **(b)**. (Rape was removed from s. 9 by the Sexual Offences Act 2003.)

:::es
**Dos formas**: **9(1)(a)** = **entrar** como intruso **con intención** de **hurtar**, causar **GBH** o hacer **daños**; importa la intención **al entrar**. **9(1)(b)** = **habiendo entrado** como intruso, **hurta o lo intenta**, o **causa o intenta causar GBH**. Los **daños** solo cuentan en la (a).
:::

### 2. Entry

- **Effective** entry is enough (*R v Brown* (1985) – leaning through a shop window); the old "substantial" idea (*R v Collins* (1973)) has been relaxed.
- **Partial entry** can be enough – even if the person couldn't steal anything from that position (*R v Ryan* (1996) – stuck with head and arm inside a window).
- Using an **instrument** to reach in may also amount to entry in some circumstances (⚠ VERIFICAR).

:::es
**Entrada**: basta que sea **efectiva** (*Brown*: inclinado por un escaparate), incluso **parcial** (*Ryan*: atrapado con cabeza y brazo dentro).
:::

### 3. Building or part of a building

- "Building" has its **ordinary meaning** with some **permanence** (houses, shops, sheds, offices, garages); it also includes an **inhabited vehicle or vessel** (e.g. a houseboat or a caravan someone lives in), **whether or not the person is there at the time** (s. 9(4)).
- **Part of a building**: a person can lawfully be in one part and be a trespasser in another (*R v Walkington* (1979) – going behind a shop counter to the till).

:::es
**Edificio**: con cierta **permanencia** (casa, tienda, cobertizo, garaje); incluye **vehículos o embarcaciones habitados** (caravana donde vive alguien), aunque no haya nadie en ese momento (s. 9(4)). **Parte de un edificio**: puedes estar legalmente en la tienda y ser intruso detrás del **mostrador** (*Walkington*).
:::

### 4. As a trespasser

- Entering **without permission** (or exceeding permission), **knowing** or being **reckless** as to that fact.
- **Exceeding permission**: a person with permission to enter for one purpose who enters for an **unlawful** purpose becomes a trespasser (*R v Jones and Smith* (1976) – son with general permission to visit his father's house entered to steal TVs).

:::es
**Como intruso**: entrar **sin permiso** (o **excediéndolo**) **sabiéndolo** o con **temeridad**. Si tienes permiso para algo y entras para **delinquir**, eres intruso (*Jones and Smith*: el hijo que entró en casa del padre a robar televisores).
:::

### 5. Intent and conditional intent

For s. 9(1)(a), a **conditional intent** to steal "**anything worth stealing**" is enough (⚠ VERIFICAR AG's References Nos 1 and 2 of 1979). The prosecution need not prove what item was targeted.

### 6. Dwellings and sentencing

Burglary of a **dwelling** carries a higher maximum (**14 years**) than other buildings (**10 years**) (⚠ VERIFICAR). Burglary is usually **either-way**, but some forms are **indictable only** (e.g. dwelling burglary involving violence or the threat of it; a **third domestic burglary** with mandatory minimum provisions ⚠ VERIFICAR).

## Police scenarios

1. **The shopper behind the counter.** KIM is a customer in a shop, then slips behind the staff counter to take cash from the till. → **Trespasser in part of a building** (*Walkington*) → s. 9(1)(b).
2. **The son.** TOM has permission to visit his mother's house; he goes in at night to steal her jewellery. → **Exceeding permission** → trespasser (*Jones and Smith*).
3. **The fight inside.** A man breaks into a house to sleep (no intent). Inside he is confronted and causes **GBH** to the owner. → Not (a) at entry, but **s. 9(1)(b)** (inflicts GBH after entering as a trespasser).
4. **The vandal.** A man enters a school intending to smash computers. → **s. 9(1)(a)** (intent to do unlawful damage).

## Common mistakes & exam traps

- **s. 9(1)(a)** intent must exist **at entry**; **s. 9(1)(b)** is about what happens **after**.
- **Unlawful damage** is only in **(a)**.
- **Inhabited caravan** = building even if nobody is in it at the time.
- **Exceeding permission** = trespasser.
- **Partial/effective entry** is enough.

## Summary

1. s. 9(1)(a): enters as trespasser with intent to steal, inflict GBH or do unlawful damage.
2. s. 9(1)(b): having entered as trespasser, steals/attempts or inflicts/attempts GBH.
3. Entry: effective, even partial (*Brown*, *Ryan*).
4. Building includes part of a building (*Walkington*) and inhabited vehicles/vessels.
5. Trespasser: knowing/reckless; exceeding permission (*Jones and Smith*); dwelling = higher maximum.
