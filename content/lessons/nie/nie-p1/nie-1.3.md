## Objectives

- Explain what **actus reus** is and its three parts: **conduct, circumstances and consequences**.
- Know **when a failure to act (omission)** can make someone guilty, and the six main **duties to act**.
- Apply the rules of **causation** (factual and legal) and spot what **breaks the chain**.

## Explanation

### 1. What is actus reus?

Most offences have two parts: **actus reus** (the "guilty act") and **mens rea** (the "guilty mind"). The actus reus is **everything in the definition of the offence except the state of mind**. It is usually made of:

- **Conduct** – what the defendant does (or fails to do). Example: *appropriating* property in theft.
- **Circumstances** – facts that must exist. Example: the property *belongs to another*.
- **Consequences (result)** – what must happen. Example: in murder, the victim *dies*.

Some offences are **conduct crimes** (the conduct is enough, e.g. perjury, dangerous driving). Others are **result crimes** (a result must happen, e.g. murder, criminal damage). Only result crimes need **causation**.

The conduct must normally be **voluntary**. If the body moves without control of the mind (e.g. a reflex, a seizure, being pushed), there is no voluntary act.

:::es
**Actus reus** = todo lo que pide la definición del delito **menos** el estado mental. Tiene tres piezas:
- **Conducta**: lo que hace (o deja de hacer) la persona.
- **Circunstancias**: hechos que tienen que darse (p. ej., que la cosa sea *de otra persona*).
- **Consecuencias/resultado**: lo que tiene que pasar (p. ej., la muerte en el asesinato).

Delitos **de mera conducta** (basta con la conducta) vs. delitos **de resultado** (hace falta un resultado y por eso hay que probar la **causalidad**). La conducta debe ser **voluntaria**: un espasmo o que te empujen no cuenta.
:::

### 2. "State of affairs" offences

A few offences are committed just by **being** in a situation, even if the defendant did not choose it. Two classic cases:

- **R v Larsonneur (1933)** – a foreign national was brought into the UK by the police against her will and was convicted of "being found" in the UK.
- **Winzar v Chief Constable of Kent (1983)** – a drunk man was taken out of a hospital onto the road by police and was convicted of being **found drunk on a highway**.

:::es
Hay delitos de **"situación"**: basta con *estar* en esa situación, aunque no lo hayas elegido. En *Winzar* (1983) la policía sacó a un borracho del hospital a la calle y aun así fue condenado por "ser encontrado borracho en la vía pública". Son excepcionales.
:::

### 3. Omissions: when is NOT acting a crime?

The general rule in English law: **there is no general duty to act**. You commit no offence by watching a stranger drown. But a person **can** be liable for an omission when they have a **duty to act**. The main duties:

| Duty arises from… | Key case | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Statute** | e.g. failing to provide a specimen, failing to stop after an accident | An Act creates the duty |
| **Contract** | *R v Pittwood* (1902) | A railway gate-keeper left the gate open; a person was killed |
| **Special relationship** | *R v Gibbins and Proctor* (1918) | Parents/carers must feed and care for a child |
| **Voluntary assumption of care** | *R v Stone and Dobinson* (1977); *R v Instan* (1893) | You took on caring for someone who cannot look after themselves |
| **Creating a dangerous situation** | *R v Miller* (1983); *R v Evans* (2009) | You start a danger (even by accident) → you must act to limit it once you realise |
| **Public office** | *R v Dytham* (1979) | A police officer watched a man being beaten to death and did nothing (misconduct in public office) |

Remember: the omission must still **cause** the result, and the defendant must have the required **mens rea**.

:::es
**Regla general:** no hay un deber general de actuar (no es delito ver a un desconocido ahogarse). **Excepciones** – hay deber de actuar si nace de:
1. **La ley** (p. ej. no parar tras un accidente).
2. **Un contrato** (*Pittwood*: el guardabarreras que dejó la barrera abierta).
3. **Una relación especial** (padres e hijos: *Gibbins and Proctor*).
4. **Asumir voluntariamente el cuidado** de alguien (*Stone and Dobinson*, *Instan*).
5. **Haber creado el peligro** (*Miller*: se quedó dormido con un cigarro, vio el fuego y se fue a otra habitación; *Evans*: suministró la heroína y no pidió ayuda).
6. **Un cargo público** (*Dytham*: un policía que miró cómo mataban a un hombre y no hizo nada).
:::

### 4. Causation

For **result crimes**, the prosecution must prove that the defendant **caused** the result. There are two tests and **both** must be met.

**a) Factual causation – the "but for" test.** *But for* the defendant's conduct, would the result have happened? In ***R v White* (1910)** the defendant poisoned his mother's drink, but she died of a heart attack before the poison worked. He was **not** the factual cause of her death (he was guilty of **attempted** murder).

**b) Legal causation.** The defendant's conduct must be an **operating and substantial cause** – it does **not** have to be the only or main cause; it must be **more than minimal** (*R v Pagett* (1983); *R v Smith* (1959)).

**The chain of causation can be broken by a *novus actus interveniens* (a new intervening act):**

- **Medical treatment** – rarely breaks the chain. Only if it is **so independent and so potent** that the original wound is merely the setting (***R v Cheshire* (1991)**). In ***R v Jordan* (1956)** the chain was broken because the wound had **almost healed** and the treatment was "palpably wrong" – treat *Jordan* as **very exceptional**. In ***R v Smith* (1959)** poor treatment did **not** break the chain because the stab wound was still an operating cause.
- **The victim's own reaction** – if the victim escapes and is hurt, the chain is broken only if the reaction was **so daft** that no reasonable person could foresee it (***R v Roberts* (1971)**; *R v Williams* (1992)).
- **Thin skull rule** – you **take your victim as you find them** (***R v Blaue* (1975)**: a Jehovah's Witness refused a blood transfusion and died; the stabber was still the cause).
- **Free, deliberate and informed act of a third party (or the victim)** – usually breaks the chain. In ***R v Kennedy (No. 2)* (2007)** the defendant prepared heroin and handed the syringe to the victim, who **injected himself** and died. The victim's free and informed choice broke the chain: Kennedy was **not** guilty of manslaughter.
- **Reasonable acts of self-defence or duty by others** do **not** break the chain: in ***R v Pagett* (1983)** the defendant used his pregnant girlfriend as a human shield and shot at police; police returned fire and killed her. Pagett caused her death.

:::es
En los delitos de resultado hay que probar la **causalidad**, con **dos** pruebas:
- **Causalidad de hecho ("but for")**: sin la conducta, ¿habría ocurrido el resultado? (*White*: envenenó a su madre, pero murió de un infarto antes de que hiciera efecto → no causó la muerte; culpable de **tentativa**).
- **Causalidad jurídica**: la conducta tiene que ser una causa **operativa y sustancial**, **más que mínima**. No hace falta que sea la única.

**¿Qué rompe la cadena?** (*novus actus interveniens*)
- **Tratamiento médico**: casi nunca. Solo si es tan independiente y potente que la herida original ya no importa (*Cheshire*). *Jordan* (herida casi curada) es una excepción rarísima.
- **Reacción de la víctima** (huir y lesionarse): solo si fue **absurda e imprevisible** (*Roberts*).
- **"Thin skull rule"**: la víctima se toma **como es** (*Blaue*: Testigo de Jehová que rechazó la transfusión → el agresor sigue siendo la causa).
- **Acto libre, deliberado e informado** de un tercero o de la víctima: suele romperla (*Kennedy*: la víctima se inyectó ella misma la heroína).
- **Reacciones razonables** de otros (defensa propia, policía) **no** la rompen (*Pagett*: usó a su novia como escudo humano).
:::

### 5. People who help: principals and accessories ⚠

> ⚠ **VERIFICAR**: en algunas ediciones este contenido está en el capítulo 1.3; en otras, en un capítulo aparte. Comprueba el índice de tu edición 2027.

- The **principal** is the person who directly carries out the actus reus.
- An **accessory** (secondary party) **aids, abets, counsels or procures** the offence and is tried and punished **as if they were a principal** (Accessories and Abettors Act 1861, s. 8 for indictable offences; Magistrates' Courts Act 1980, s. 44 for summary offences).
- ***R v Jogee* (2016)**: the accessory must **intend to assist or encourage** the principal to commit the crime (with knowledge of the essential facts). **Foresight** that the principal *might* commit it is only **evidence** of intention – it is not enough on its own.
- **Innocent agent**: if the person who does the act is innocent (e.g. a child under 10 or someone tricked), the person who used them is the **principal**.
- **Withdrawal** must be **timely and effective**; usually it must be **communicated** to the others – just walking away silently is normally not enough.

:::es
- **Autor (principal)**: quien realiza el actus reus.
- **Partícipe (accessory)**: quien **ayuda, instiga, aconseja o provoca** (*aid, abet, counsel or procure*). Se le juzga y castiga **como al autor**.
- *Jogee* (2016): el partícipe debe **querer ayudar o animar** al autor; **prever** que podría pasar es solo **indicio** de intención, no basta por sí solo.
- **Agente inocente**: si quien actúa es inocente (p. ej. un niño menor de 10), quien lo usa es el autor.
- **Desistimiento**: tiene que ser a tiempo y eficaz, normalmente **comunicado**; irse sin decir nada no suele bastar.
:::

## Police scenarios

1. **The babysitter.** AYLA agrees (unpaid, as a favour) to look after her elderly neighbour, who cannot leave bed. After a week she stops visiting and the neighbour dies of dehydration. → AYLA **voluntarily assumed care** (*Stone and Dobinson*). Her omission can be the actus reus of manslaughter.
2. **The cigarette.** MARK falls asleep and his cigarette sets fire to a sofa in a flat he is renting. He wakes, sees the fire, and moves to another room without doing anything. → He **created the danger** and failed to act (*Miller*): the actus reus of arson (criminal damage by fire) is complete.
3. **The ambulance.** KAI stabs VERA. In hospital the doctors give her the wrong drug and she dies. The wound is still serious. → Medical negligence **does not** break the chain while the wound is still an **operating and substantial cause** (*Smith*, *Cheshire*).
4. **The jump.** A man attacks his passenger in a moving car; she jumps out and is injured. → Her reaction was foreseeable, not "daft": the attacker caused the injuries (*Roberts*).

:::es
1. **La vecina**: asumió voluntariamente su cuidado → su omisión cuenta.
2. **El cigarro**: creó el peligro y no hizo nada → responde (*Miller*).
3. **Error médico**: no rompe la cadena si la herida sigue siendo causa operativa y sustancial.
4. **Salto del coche**: reacción previsible → el agresor causó las lesiones (*Roberts*).
:::

## Common mistakes & exam traps

- **"No duty to act" is the general rule.** Exam questions often describe a stranger who watches and does nothing → **no liability** unless one of the duties applies.
- **"Operating and substantial" ≠ "main" or "only" cause.** It only needs to be **more than minimal**.
- **Medical treatment almost never breaks the chain** – choose *Jordan* only if the wound had **healed / was no longer operating**.
- **Thin skull rule includes beliefs** (religion in *Blaue*), not just physical weakness.
- **Kennedy (No. 2)**: *supplying* drugs which the adult victim **self-injects** freely → chain broken. Contrast with **Evans**: the supplier who **then** realises the danger and does nothing can be liable through the **Miller** duty.
- **Foresight ≠ intention** for accessories after *Jogee*.
- Watch the words **"aid, abet, counsel or procure"** – an accessory is **punished like the principal**.
- 🇪🇸 *False friend*: **"actual"** bodily harm no significa "actual", sino **real**.

## Summary

1. Actus reus = conduct + circumstances + consequences (everything except the mental element).
2. Conduct must be voluntary; "state of affairs" offences are rare exceptions (*Larsonneur*, *Winzar*).
3. No general duty to act; duties come from statute, contract, relationship, assumed care, creating danger, public office.
4. Causation: "but for" (*White*) **and** an operating and substantial (more than minimal) cause (*Smith*, *Pagett*).
5. Chain broken only by a free, deliberate, informed act (*Kennedy*), a "daft" reaction (*Roberts*) or overwhelming medical treatment (*Jordan*, exceptional); thin skull rule applies (*Blaue*).
