## Objectives

- Distinguish **intention** (direct and oblique) from **recklessness** and **negligence**.
- Understand the special words used in offences: **maliciously, wilfully, knowingly, dishonestly**.
- Apply **strict liability**, **transferred mens rea** and the **coincidence** of actus reus and mens rea.

## Explanation

### 1. What is mens rea?

**Mens rea** is the **state of mind** the prosecution must prove for an offence. Each offence has its own: you must read the **words** of the offence (e.g. "intentionally", "recklessly", "knowing or believing", "dishonestly"). The mens rea must relate to **each element** of the actus reus unless the law says otherwise.

:::es
**Mens rea** = el **estado mental** que exige cada delito. Cada delito tiene el suyo: mira las **palabras** de la definición ("intentionally", "recklessly", "knowing or believing", "dishonestly"…).
:::

### 2. Intention

- **Direct intent**: the result is the defendant's **aim or purpose**. It does not matter how unlikely the result is.
- **Oblique (indirect) intent**: the result is **not** the aim, but the jury **may find** intention where the result was a **virtual certainty** (barring some unforeseen intervention) of the defendant's actions **and** the defendant **appreciated** that this was the case (***R v Woollin* (1998)**, building on *R v Nedrick* (1986) and *R v Moloney* (1985)).
- **Criminal Justice Act 1967, s. 8**: a court or jury is **not bound** to infer that a person intended or foresaw a result just because it was a natural and probable consequence; they decide by looking at **all the evidence**.
- **Specific vs basic intent** (useful for intoxication – *DPP v Majewski* (1977)): offences of **specific intent** (e.g. murder, s. 18 wounding with intent, theft) need intention; voluntary intoxication **can** be relevant to whether the defendant formed it. Offences of **basic intent** (e.g. s. 20, ABH, common assault) can be committed recklessly; voluntary intoxication is **no defence**.

:::es
- **Intención directa**: el resultado es su **objetivo**. Da igual que fuera improbable.
- **Intención oblicua/indirecta**: no era su objetivo, pero el jurado **puede** apreciar intención si el resultado era **prácticamente seguro** y el acusado **se daba cuenta** (*Woollin*). Ojo: el jurado "puede", no "debe".
- **CJA 1967 s. 8**: el jurado **no está obligado** a deducir la intención solo porque el resultado fuera natural y probable; mira toda la prueba.
- **Specific intent** (asesinato, s. 18, hurto): la embriaguez voluntaria puede impedir formar la intención. **Basic intent** (s. 20, ABH, agresión simple): basta la temeridad; la embriaguez voluntaria **no** sirve de defensa (*Majewski*).
:::

### 3. Recklessness

Since ***R v G* (2003)** recklessness is **subjective**. A person acts recklessly with respect to:

- a **circumstance** when they are **aware of a risk** that it exists or will exist; and
- a **result** when they are **aware of a risk** that it will occur;

**and** it is, in the circumstances **known to them**, **unreasonable to take the risk**.

The question is what **this defendant** actually foresaw – not what a reasonable person would have foreseen. (*R v G*: two boys aged 11 and 12 set fire to newspapers behind a shop; they did not foresee the risk of the fire spreading → **not reckless**.)

**"Maliciously"** (e.g. Offences Against the Person Act 1861, s. 20) means **intention or subjective recklessness** (***R v Cunningham* (1957)**). For s. 20 it is enough that the defendant foresaw that **some harm** might result – not necessarily serious harm (***R v Savage; DPP v Parmenter* (1991)**).

:::es
Desde *R v G* (2003) la temeridad es **subjetiva**: la persona **era consciente** de un riesgo y, según lo que **ella sabía**, era **irrazonable** asumirlo. Importa lo que previó **el acusado**, no lo que habría previsto una persona razonable (en *R v G*, dos niños de 11 y 12 años no previeron que el fuego se extendería → no hubo temeridad).

**"Maliciously"** = intención **o** temeridad subjetiva (*Cunningham*). Para la s. 20 basta prever **algún daño**, no un daño grave (*Savage; Parmenter*).
:::

### 4. Other mental words

- **Negligence** – falling **below the standard of a reasonable person**. It is **objective** (what the defendant should have known). Used for some offences, e.g. careless driving and gross negligence manslaughter.
- **Wilfully** – **deliberately or recklessly** (***R v Sheppard* (1981)**, neglect of a child).
- **Knowing** – actual knowledge; also **"wilful blindness"**: deliberately shutting your eyes to an obvious truth.
- **Believing** – less than knowing but more than suspecting (important in **handling stolen goods**).
- **Dishonestly** – ***Ivey v Genting Casinos* (2017)**, confirmed for criminal cases by ***R v Barton and Booth* (2020)**: (1) What was the **defendant's actual state of knowledge or belief** as to the facts? (2) Given that, was the conduct dishonest by the standards of **ordinary decent people**? The defendant does **not** have to realise that ordinary people would see it as dishonest (the old *Ghosh* second limb is gone).

:::es
- **Negligencia**: estar por **debajo del estándar de una persona razonable** (objetivo).
- **Wilfully**: deliberadamente **o** con temeridad (*Sheppard*).
- **Knowing**: saber de verdad (incluye "ceguera voluntaria").
- **Believing**: menos que saber, más que sospechar (clave en receptación).
- **Dishonestly** (*Ivey*, confirmado por *Barton and Booth*): 1) ¿qué sabía o creía **realmente** el acusado? 2) Con eso, ¿es deshonesto según las **personas normales y decentes**? Ya **no** hace falta que el acusado se dé cuenta de que la gente lo vería deshonesto (adiós *Ghosh*).
:::

### 5. Strict liability

Some offences do **not** need mens rea for **at least one** element of the actus reus. These are offences of **strict liability** – common in regulatory areas (road traffic, food, licensing).

- There is a **presumption** that Parliament intended mens rea for criminal offences (***Sweet v Parsley* (1970)**; ***B (A Minor) v DPP* (2000)**). The presumption can be displaced by the **words** of the statute or by **necessary implication**.
- **Absolute liability** (rare): no mens rea at all and not even a voluntary act is needed (see *Winzar* in 1.3).

:::es
**Responsabilidad objetiva (strict liability)**: no hace falta mens rea en **al menos un** elemento (típico en tráfico, alimentación, licencias). Hay una **presunción** de que el Parlamento quiere mens rea (*Sweet v Parsley*, *B v DPP*); solo se rompe por las palabras de la ley o por implicación necesaria.
:::

### 6. Transferred mens rea

If A intends to hit B but misses and hits C, the intention **transfers** to C (***R v Latimer* (1886)** – a belt aimed at one man hit a woman).

It **only transfers to an offence of the same kind**: in ***R v Pembliton* (1874)** a stone thrown at people broke a window – the intention to hurt people did **not** transfer to criminal damage (but think about **recklessness** as to the window).

:::es
**Mens rea transferida**: si apuntas a B y das a C, tu intención se "traslada" a C (*Latimer*). Solo entre delitos **del mismo tipo**: tirar una piedra a personas y romper una ventana no traslada la intención de herir a los daños (*Pembliton*) – aunque quizá hubo **temeridad** respecto a la ventana.
:::

### 7. Coincidence of actus reus and mens rea

The mens rea and actus reus must **coincide in time**, but the courts are flexible:

- **Continuing act** – ***Fagan v MPC* (1969)**: Fagan accidentally drove onto a police officer's foot, then **refused to move** once he knew. The act continued, and the mens rea joined it → assault (battery).
- **One series of acts / one transaction** – ***Thabo Meli v R* (1954)**; ***R v Church* (1966)**: if the defendant had mens rea at some point during a series of acts that together caused death, that is enough.

:::es
Actus reus y mens rea deben **coincidir en el tiempo**, con flexibilidad:
- **Acto continuado** (*Fagan*): le pisó el pie a un policía con el coche sin querer y luego **se negó a moverse** → la intención se une al acto que sigue.
- **Serie de actos / una misma operación** (*Thabo Meli*, *Church*): basta con que hubiera mens rea en algún momento de la secuencia.
:::

## Police scenarios

1. **The arsonist.** DREW sets fire to a house to claim the insurance. He knows the owner is asleep inside and that death is virtually certain, although he hopes she escapes. She dies. → **Oblique intent** may be found (*Woollin*): the jury **may** find he intended death.
2. **The bottle.** TOM throws a bottle at RIA in a pub; she ducks and it hits SAM. → TOM's intent **transfers** to SAM (*Latimer*).
3. **Not knowing the risk.** A 12-year-old lights a bin; he genuinely did not think the fire could spread. → **Not reckless** (*R v G*): subjective test.
4. **The foot.** A driver stops on an officer's foot by accident and, when told, says "wait" and turns the engine off. → **Continuing act** (*Fagan*).

:::es
1. **Incendio para cobrar el seguro**: muerte prácticamente segura y él lo sabía → el jurado **puede** apreciar intención (*Woollin*).
2. **La botella**: la intención se traslada a la víctima real (*Latimer*).
3. **Niño que no previó el riesgo**: no hay temeridad (*R v G*).
4. **El pie del policía**: acto continuado (*Fagan*).
:::

## Common mistakes & exam traps

- **Woollin**: the jury **may find** intention – it is **not** a rule that they **must**. Virtual certainty is **evidence** of intention.
- **Recklessness is subjective** (since *R v G*): the question is "did **this** defendant foresee the risk?", never "would a reasonable person?".
- **s. 20 "maliciously"**: foresight of **some harm**, not serious harm.
- **Transferred malice does not cross between different types of offence** (*Pembliton*).
- **Dishonesty after Ivey**: no need for the defendant to realise ordinary people would think it dishonest.
- **Strict liability** does not mean "no mens rea for anything" – only for **at least one** element.
- 🇪🇸 *False friend*: **"wilful"** = deliberado (no "voluntarioso").

## Summary

1. Mens rea = the mental element; read the exact words of each offence.
2. Intention: aim/purpose (direct) or virtual certainty + appreciation (oblique, *Woollin*) – the jury **may** find it.
3. Recklessness is subjective: aware of the risk + unreasonable to take it (*R v G*); "maliciously" = intent or recklessness (*Cunningham*).
4. Dishonesty: *Ivey* two-stage test; strict liability needs no mens rea for at least one element (*Sweet v Parsley* presumption).
5. Transferred mens rea only for the same type of offence (*Latimer* vs *Pembliton*); AR and MR must coincide (*Fagan*, *Thabo Meli*).
